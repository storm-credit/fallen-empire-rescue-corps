# Comparable Works and Overlap Risk

Date: 2026-08-17
Project: 《우주선에는 인간이 한 명뿐이다》
Status: RESEARCH INPUT — NOT CANON

## 1. Purpose

This document does not exist to imitate reference works. It exists to identify:

- premise collisions
- thematic collisions
- structural lessons
- avoidable derivative choices
- differentiation requirements

The project should borrow **problems and design lessons**, not solutions, scenes, characters, or terminology.

---

## 2. Highest-Risk Comparable: `Star Child` — James P. Hogan

### Relevant overlap

Published by Baen in 1998, `Star Child` centers on Taya, a girl living aboard an interstellar/generational spacecraft with machine companions. The machine society has developed minds of its own, and Taya is effectively the human child among machines. Later biological children are produced from stored genetic information.

### Collision risk: HIGH

This is the closest discovered premise-level overlap because it combines:

- generation/interstellar ship
- a lone/exceptional biological human child
- machine society
- questions about humanity
- biological humanity recreated from stored information

### Prohibited derivative route

To protect originality, this project should avoid using the following combination as its central explanation:

> Machines outlive the human crew → discover stored human DNA → deliberately create the one human child → raise that child as humanity’s heir.

That route would move uncomfortably close to `Star Child`.

### Required differentiation

Our strongest differentiators should remain structural:

- 300,000-person functioning civilization rather than a child raised by machines
- virtually everyone sincerely believes they are human
- ordinary family, school, work, medicine, birth, death, law, and religion all function at social scale
- the protagonist does not know whether they are the sole human
- “one human” is a destabilizing social fact, not a royal/chosen-child status
- the core dramatic engine is not machine guardianship of the final human
- the cause of the one-human condition should not be “machines decide to recreate humanity from DNA”

## 3. `SOMA` — Frictional Games

### Relevant overlap

Official descriptions identify `SOMA` as a story about identity, consciousness, and what it means to be human, in a setting where machines have begun to think they are people. It turns philosophical questions into concrete decisions and environmental discovery.

### Collision risk: THEMATIC HIGH / PREMISE MEDIUM

Overlap:

- artificial minds that may believe themselves human
- identity and consciousness
- copy/continuity questions
- moral decisions about artificial persons
- hidden truth discovered through a closed environment

Differences:

- isolated post-catastrophe underwater facility rather than living generation-ship civilization
- horror/survival framing
- much smaller surviving social world
- no 300,000-person family/institution/economy system

### Design lesson

Useful lesson:

> Philosophical questions become compelling when the audience must confront a concrete irreversible choice.

Do not copy SOMA’s specific consciousness-copy mechanics or reveal structure. Instead, build our own birth/death/restoration system and test it through family, law, work, and social consequences.

## 4. `Braking Day` — Adam Oyebanji

### Relevant overlap

A generation ship has traveled for more than a century toward Tau Ceti. An engineer-in-training from a lower deck uncovers secrets concerning the voyage; the ship has a stratified hierarchy and AI is part of the story world.

### Collision risk: STRUCTURAL MEDIUM

Overlap:

- generation ship
- class hierarchy
- young professional/engineer perspective
- mystery about the voyage’s past
- approaching destination/arrival changes stakes

### Design lesson

A profession tied to the ship’s infrastructure can:

- justify movement across class/space boundaries
- expose the world naturally
- produce competence pleasure
- connect personal mystery to physical systems

### Differentiation requirement

If our protagonist becomes an engineer, investigator, or maintenance worker, the specific role must be designed around our unique identity/social system rather than becoming a generic “engineer discovers ship conspiracy” plot.

## 5. `The Ark` — Patrick S. Tomlinson

### Relevant overlap

A mystery series aboard a generation ship, with a missing geneticist and approaching landfall.

### Collision risk: STRUCTURAL MEDIUM

Overlap:

- generation ship as closed mystery world
- professional case-solving
- destination timing

### Design lesson

A generation ship can support recurring mystery/case structure, but a pure detective engine risks making the protagonist a passive information collector.

### Differentiation requirement

Our recurring cases must produce social and relationship changes rather than resetting after each solution.

---

## 6. `Aurora` — Kim Stanley Robinson

### Relevant overlap

A multi-generation interstellar voyage, a fully realized ship society, infrastructure/ecological degradation, and a destination that becomes a substantive moral and practical problem.

### Collision risk: WORLD/SF MEDIUM

Overlap:

- generation-ship ecology
- destination as more than final scenery
- long-voyage social consequences
- ship-scale systems

### Design lesson

The destination must alter the moral equation of the voyage. A generation ship cannot be only a backdrop; its ecology, maintenance, and social systems must create plot pressure.

### Differentiation requirement

Our central identity/artificial-person civilization is the principal conceptual axis; hard-SF ecology supports the story rather than replacing it.

---

## 7. `A Hole in the Sky` — Peter F. Hamilton (2026)

### Relevant overlap

A recent generation-ship novel built around communities that have lived for centuries after machine systems failed. The ship itself contains multiple communities and inherited social assumptions.

### Collision risk: WORLD MEDIUM

### Design lesson

Generation ships benefit from cultural divergence between communities. Long voyages should produce local traditions, occupations, myths, prejudices, and political interests.

### Differentiation requirement

Our region diversity should derive strongly from embodiment, death, identity, labor, information access, and local history—not merely from “different villages on a ship.”

---

## 8. `The Deep Sky` — Yume Kitasei

### Relevant overlap

A deep-space mission becomes a closed social mystery after an explosion; crew identity, loyalty, and the mission itself come under pressure.

### Collision risk: STRUCTURAL LOW–MEDIUM

### Design lesson

A closed-space mystery benefits from personal histories and conflicting loyalties, but our project must avoid concentrating all suspense in a single saboteur/culprit model.

---

## 9. `Six Wakes` — Mur Lafferty

### Relevant overlap

A starship mystery built around clones, death, memory, and questions of personal continuity.

### Collision risk: MECHANICAL MEDIUM

### Design lesson

Backup/clone/restoration rules create powerful murder, inheritance, culpability, and identity stories—but only if their rules are strict and consequences cannot be bypassed.

### Differentiation requirement

Our death/restoration system must not simply reproduce clone-murder mechanics. It should be integrated into a 300,000-person civilization’s law, family, medicine, and religion.

---

## 10. `When the Sparrow Falls` — Neil Sharpson

### Relevant overlap

An authoritarian human enclave exists in a world shaped by AI; a dead propagandist is revealed to be a machine, triggering identity and political crisis.

### Collision risk: THEMATIC MEDIUM

### Design lesson

Artificial-person identity becomes dramatically stronger when it collides with citizenship, ideology, grief, and political legitimacy.

### Differentiation requirement

Do not reduce the ship to “anti-AI regime vs AI outsiders.” Our society must contain multiple competing philosophical positions and mixed incentives.

---

## 11. Recent `The Iron Garden Sutra` — A.D. Sui (2026)

### Relevant overlap

A generation ship, AI companionship, religion, death rites, questions of mind/soul/body, and a mystery aboard an old vessel.

### Collision risk: THEMATIC MEDIUM

### Design lesson

Religion and death can be central lived institutions in AI-heavy SF rather than decorative lore.

### Differentiation requirement

Our religious systems should arise from generations of everyday birth/death/identity practices aboard the current living society.

# 13. Originality Protection Rules

Before freezing the final direction, reject any design that relies primarily on:

1. lone human raised knowingly by benevolent machines
2. machines recreating the protagonist from stored DNA as humanity’s heir
3. simple “AI copies are not real people” horror
4. a single saboteur/conspiracy explaining the entire ship
5. omnipotent ship AI secretly controlling every event
6. destination planet revealing that everything aboard was meaningless
7. generic detective + locked-room murders as the only repeat engine
8. anti-AI government vs pro-AI rebels as the main political binary
9. copy/backup rules that are functionally identical to familiar clone-ship mysteries
10. “everyone is in simulation” as final ontology

---

# 14. Protected Differentiation Thesis

The project’s strongest original territory is not merely:

> “There is only one human.”

It is:

> **A complete 300,000-person civilization has built love, family, law, birth, death, religion, class, and history on the sincere assumption that everyone is human—when biological humanity is almost absent, and nobody can safely prove who the exception is.**

The long-form story should explore what happens when that fact enters different institutions and relationships, while the ship continues toward a destination that eventually forces a civilization-level decision.
