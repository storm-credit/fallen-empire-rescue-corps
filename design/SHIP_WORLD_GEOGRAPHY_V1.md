# Ship-World Geography v1

Status: PROVISIONAL WORLD DESIGN — NOT FROZEN
Project: 《우주선에는 인간이 한 명뿐이다》

## 1. World Design Goal

The generation ship must not feel like:

- one endless metal corridor
- one city with different room labels
- a military vessel enlarged to city size
- a collection of disposable “biomes” visited once

It should feel like **an archipelago/continent compressed into one machine**, where geography produces culture, economy, class, politics, memory, and distinct beliefs about personhood.

---

# 2. Macroform

## 2.1 Connected habitat archipelago

Working physical concept:

- 8 large rotating habitat drums/rings
- one non-rotating axial service/transit spine
- forward navigation/observation structures
- aft power/propulsion complex
- external radiator and maintenance structures
- industrial/recycling volumes outside the main living landscapes

All are parts of **one physically connected generation ship**, not a fleet.

## 2.2 Why rotation

The ship avoids artificial-gravity handwaving by using rotation for most residential gravity.

Consequences:

- normal gravity in habitat worlds
- low/zero gravity near the axis
- transition sickness and training for residents moving between environments
- distinctive axial labor culture
- physical travel feels meaningful

## 2.3 Scale target

Keep exact dimensions open until engineering QA.

Narrative target:

- hundreds of square kilometers of primary habitat surface
- additional stacked agricultural/industrial volume
- enough room for dense cities, water, parks, managed ecosystems, and low-density settlements
- total population around 300,000

The world should feel large enough that ordinary citizens can spend a lifetime without visiting every habitat.

---

# 3. Infrastructure Logic

## 3.1 Power

Provisional:

- fusion power as primary civilization-scale source
- smaller long-duration fission/isotope systems for emergency black-start and isolated systems
- propulsion and city power are related but not identical systems

Most of the voyage is cruise/coast rather than constant maximum-thrust operation.

## 3.2 Heat

Large external radiator systems are essential.

Radiator damage or coolant politics can create plot pressure because heat rejection is as important as power generation.

## 3.3 Air / water / nutrients

Closed loops are never literally perfect.

The ship survives through:

- aggressive water recovery
- atmosphere scrubbing
- nitrogen/carbon/phosphorus accounting
- microbial recycling
- material reserves
- strict contamination control

Loss over centuries is a political issue, not a solved background detail.

## 3.4 Food

Avoid the implausible idea that all 300,000 people eat from picturesque fields alone.

Use a mixed system:

- visible agriculture for fruit/vegetables/cultural food
- high-yield stacked cultivation
- fungal/microbial protein
- algae/fermentation
- cultured animal products where culturally valued
- seed/genetic libraries

Food therefore has both industrial and cultural dimensions.

## 3.5 Material economy

Nothing is truly “thrown away.”

Important social concept:

> Waste is displaced inventory.

The ship tracks:

- metals
- polymers
- organics
- rare catalysts
- electronics
- coolant mass
- structural feedstock

Repairing one district can mean denying material to another.

This creates politics without needing war.

## 3.6 Maintenance

Centuries of repair mean the ship is no longer technologically uniform.

It contains:

- original launch-era structures
- standardized replacement eras
- locally improvised repairs
- obsolete interfaces
- abandoned utilities still embedded behind newer systems

The ship itself has archaeological layers.

---

# 4. Transit

## 4.1 Axial transit

Major habitat worlds connect to the central spine through radial lift/transfer systems.

A fast axial transit line moves people and cargo longitudinally.

Travel between habitats is easy enough for commerce but inconvenient enough to preserve local culture.

## 4.2 Social consequence

Travel requires:

- gravity transition
- scheduling
- security/customs in some periods
- cargo allocation
- time

Therefore “I have never been to Habitat Six” is socially believable.

## 4.3 Narrative consequence

The protagonist’s profession must provide a real reason to cross these boundaries.

Ordinary citizens should not wander the entire ship without purpose.

# 5. Habitat Worlds

Final names are not selected. Functional labels are used to avoid premature aesthetic lock-in.

---

## H1 — CIVIC CORE

### Original function

Governance, higher education, law, diplomacy between habitats, shipwide civil coordination.

### Present character

Dense, formal, information-rich, high trust in standardized records and institutions.

### Economy

- administration
- law
- research
- education
- finance/accounting
- coordination services

### Class structure

Prestige comes from credentials, institutional networks, and access to central decision-making rather than raw wealth alone.

### Daily life

Fast public transit, compact housing, public forums, major universities, bureaucratic rituals.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Civic humanism:**

> A human is a recognized member of the civic order.

This habitat is most likely to argue that biology should not determine rights—while also quietly benefiting from the institutions that define recognition.

### Story value

- law
- policy
- media
- institutional secrecy
- public hearings
- protests
- elite family pressure

### Risk

Could become generic capital city. Must have ship-specific architecture and dependency on other habitats.

---

## H2 — BIOSPHERE COMMONS

### Original function

Ecology, food diversity, seed libraries, biological recycling, large green/water environments.

### Present character

Lower density, strong place-based communities, visible seasonal rituals even though seasons are engineered.

### Economy

- specialty crops
- seed/genetic stewardship
- ecological maintenance
- fermentation
- water-cycle work
- cultural food production

### Class structure

Status attaches to long stewardship, ecological expertise, family/community continuity, and trust.

### Daily life

More communal work, food rituals, outdoor spaces, local festivals tied to artificial seasons.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Life-cycle humanism:**

> To be human is to be born into, consume from, care for, die within, and return material to the living world.

This culture may distrust easy restoration because death is part of ecological reciprocity.

### Story value

- birth
- death
- ecological crisis
- food politics
- old biological records
- community memory

### Risk

Avoid “simple pastoral people vs cold city elites.” They should possess sophisticated ecology and their own power interests.

---

## H3 — FABRICATION CONCORD

### Original function

Manufacturing, recycling, toolmaking, structural fabrication, material recovery.

### Present character

Loud, shift-based, proud of repair competence, materially pragmatic.

### Economy

- metals recovery
- printing/fabrication
- machine tools
- robotics
- structural components
- industrial chemistry

### Class structure

Guild-like status emerges from competence, certification, and control of scarce fabrication capacity.

### Daily life

Work rhythms, protective clothing, apprenticeship networks, communal maintenance culture.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Continuity-through-work:**

> Things—and people—are defined less by what they were made from than by what they continue to do and whom they remain responsible to.

This culture may be unusually accepting of bodily replacement but skeptical of memory-only restoration.

### Story value

- resource conflict
- labor politics
- repair competence
- body-maintenance analogies
- sabotage false leads
- class negotiation

### Risk

Do not turn into cyberpunk factory slum stereotype.

---

## H4 — KINSHIP BELT

### Original function

High-capacity residential, reproductive medicine, childhood education, population health.

### Present character

Family-dense, multigenerational, socially intrusive, rich in local schools/clinics/community organizations.

### Economy

- medicine
- education
- childcare
- counseling
- domestic services
- reproductive support

### Class structure

Reputation attaches heavily to family networks, caregiving records, school placement, and long-term social trust.

### Daily life

Crowded public spaces, children everywhere, family events, neighborhood monitoring, strong intergenerational expectations.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Relational humanism:**

> A person becomes human through being someone’s child, parent, sibling, partner, teacher, or neighbor.

This society can become compassionate or suffocating depending on who is recognized as belonging.

### Story value

- protagonist family
- birth-system clues
- guardianship
- children
- inheritance
- intimate social pressure

### Risk

Avoid making this “the women/children habitat.” All genders and professions participate; family is an institution, not décor.

---

## H5 — EXCHANGE RING

### Original function

Cargo transfer, markets, hospitality, inter-habitat trade, redistribution.

### Present character

Cosmopolitan, mixed accents/customs, temporary residents, migrants, traders, informal brokers.

### Economy

- logistics
- warehousing
- markets
- hospitality
- insurance
- transport contracting

### Class structure

Network access, mobility rights, and information create status.

### Daily life

High turnover, public markets, travelers, identity verification, mixed cuisine, temporary housing.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Transactional/cosmopolitan humanism:**

> If you can enter contracts, bear obligations, and be held accountable, your substrate is nobody else’s business.

This can be liberating but may reduce people to legal/economic units.

### Story value

- migration
- black markets
- identity fraud
- inter-habitat politics
- rumor flow
- recurring cross-regional cast

### Risk

Avoid generic space bazaar aesthetic.

---

## H6 — MEMORY COMMONS

### Original function

Long-term records, simulation/research, arts, communication infrastructure, cognitive medicine support.

### Present character

Highly networked society where recorded memory, personal archives, media, and reputation are culturally powerful.

### Economy

- archives
- media
- cognitive services
- software
- education
- entertainment
- record authentication

### Class structure

Status attaches to archive access, provenance, cultural influence, and expertise in memory integrity.

### Daily life

Personal recording is common, historical reenactment popular, public disputes over authenticity frequent.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Memory-continuity humanism:**

> You are the continuity of the self you can remember and prove.

This habitat is likely more accepting of restoration—until a restored person contradicts the archive.

### Story value

- backup disputes
- historical clues
- propaganda
- identity proof
- media storms
- unreliable archives

### Risk

Do not make it the obvious “secret data center where all answers live.” Archives must be fragmented, political, and incomplete.

---

## H7 — LOW-LINK COMMUNITIES

### Original function

Originally ordinary habitat space, later shaped by a historical network disaster, political schism, or deliberate autonomy movement.

### Present character

Lower dependence on ubiquitous personal recording and centralized cognitive services.

This is **not anti-technology primitivism**. Residents use advanced tools but deliberately restrict certain forms of identity capture and central record dependence.

### Economy

- specialized crafts/services
- local agriculture
- privacy-sensitive work
- independent maintenance
- cultural tourism under tension

### Class structure

Status comes from face-to-face reputation, local service, and long memory held by communities rather than databases.

### Daily life

Fewer ambient records, stronger local witness culture, explicit consent around recording.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Unrecorded-self humanism:**

> A person is not reducible to what can be copied, stored, or verified.

This habitat may view backup culture as a threat to dignity.

### Story value

- information blind spots
- oral history
- witness conflicts
- political autonomy
- death/backup ethics
- fair red herrings

### Risk

Avoid romanticizing privacy culture as morally pure. It can hide abuse and exclusion as easily as it protects autonomy.

---

## H8 — LEGACY HABITAT

### Original function

One of the oldest launch-era residential/ecological habitats.

### Present character

Patchwork city layered over centuries of retrofits. Old structures remain because replacement would cost too much or destroy communities.

### Economy

- mixed ordinary services
- repair/salvage
- historical tourism
- cheap housing in old zones
- niche old-interface trades

### Class structure

Sharp micro-geographic differences: respected old families, poor maintenance districts, heritage enclaves, redevelopment interests.

### Daily life

People live physically beside sealed doors, obsolete infrastructure, old signage, inaccessible utility voids, and repurposed launch-era spaces.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Ancestral humanism:**

> We are human because we descend—culturally and historically—from the people who launched.

This can support both inclusive continuity and dangerous bloodline myth.

### Story value

- historical archaeology
- protagonist origins
- hidden infrastructure
- old medical architecture
- gentrification/redevelopment
- tangible clues from the transition era

### Risk

Do not make every old wall secretly contain a plot clue.

---

# 6. AXIAL WORLD — THE SPINE

This is not a ninth rotating habitat in the same sense.

### Function

- inter-habitat transit
- cargo transfer
- shipwide utilities
- low-gravity industrial/service work
- external maintenance access

### Population

Permanent population smaller than major habitats but socially distinct.

### Culture

Residents/workers experience the ship as machinery more directly than most habitat citizens.

They understand:

- rotation
- mass balance
- docking
- hull stress
- maintenance windows

in everyday life.

### Class issue

Habitat citizens depend on axial workers while often treating their environment as dangerous/service-class territory.

### Human/personhood belief tendency

**Embodied-pragmatic view:**

Bodies are constraints that work adapts around. This may make axial culture less attached to a single “natural” body ideal.

### Story value

- transit chokepoints
- class friction
- technical competence
- zero/low gravity
- exterior emergencies
- smuggling
- access to old service routes

---

# 7. FORWARD / AFT NON-CIVILIAN ZONES

## Forward Navigation Complex

Contains navigation, astronomy, sensor fusion, destination planning, long-baseline observations.

Prestige is high but access is limited for operational reasons, not because it is a villain lair.

## Aft Power / Propulsion Complex

High-energy, dangerous, heavily automated and professionally staffed.

Most citizens never visit.

These zones should remain workplaces, not fantasy castles.

# 8. Shipwide Politics

The habitat system naturally creates federal politics.

Questions:

- who controls shared material reserves?
- who pays for repairs?
- who gets priority during power/heat emergencies?
- how are population transfers decided?
- how much autonomy can a habitat claim?
- which records are shipwide vs local?
- who defines personhood law?
- who controls arrival planning?

No habitat is self-sufficient.

Interdependence prevents simple secession plots from being easy.

---

# 9. Why Cultures Diverged

Cultural difference should arise from five pressures:

1. original technical function
2. centuries of local crises
3. physical environment
4. labor/economic incentives
5. historical stance during the biological-to-artificial transition

The fifth item is critical.

Different habitats may preserve distorted fragments of the old conflict without knowing the full truth.

Their philosophies of humanity are therefore **historical fossils**.

---

# 10. Narrative Use Rule

A habitat is not “completed” after one Arc.

Once introduced, it must continue to influence:

- politics
- economy
- relationships
- resource flow
- faction behavior
- later crises
- public reaction

The series map should become more interconnected, not a sequence of disposable destinations.

---

# 11. Anti-Repetition Geography Rule

Do not assign one genre permanently to each habitat.

Bad:

- H2 always ecology episodes
- H3 always factory episodes
- H6 always mystery archives

Good:

The same habitat should later host different story functions because society changes.

Example pattern:

- first visit: family/culture
- later: political conflict
- later: infrastructure consequence
- later: alliance/payoff

---

# 12. Current Red-Team Questions

Before freeze:

1. Are eight habitats too neat/symmetrical?
2. Does each have enough economic interdependence?
3. Is average usable area plausible for 300,000 residents plus food systems?
4. How long does transit actually take?
5. How does rotating-habitat maintenance work over centuries?
6. How much agriculture must be visible vs industrial?
7. Are population differences large enough to create local cultures but small enough for one polity?
8. Which habitats were most altered during the artificial-successor transition?
9. Which habitat currently holds the greatest political leverage?
10. Where can the protagonist plausibly live while traveling widely?

These are required before geography freeze.
