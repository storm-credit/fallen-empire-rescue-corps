# Ending Architecture Options v1

Status: COMPARATIVE — NO ENDING FROZEN
Project: 《우주선에는 인간이 한 명뿐이다》

## 1. Ending Requirement

The ending must not merely answer:

> Who was the sole biological human?

That is a mystery payoff, not a series ending.

The final act must force the civilization to **live by an answer** to:

> What makes a person worthy of belonging, and who owns the future of a civilization inherited from biological humanity?

Every ending candidate must resolve four layers:

1. personal — protagonist / closest relationships
2. institutional — law / citizenship / privacy / family
3. historical — what the transition means
4. future — destination / ship / reproduction / mission

---

# END-A — CIVIC HUMANITY
## “Human” becomes an inherited civic identity, not a biological caste

### Core answer

> Humanity is not preserved by maintaining one substrate. It is preserved when descendants accept responsibility for one another and for the civilization they inherited.

### Institutional resolution

The civilization formally separates:

- `baseline Homo sapiens` — narrow biomedical/developmental category
- `human` — civic/historical identity available to the ship’s persons

Biological origin cannot determine civil rank.

### Sole-human resolution

The sole baseline person receives:

- privacy
- bodily autonomy
- no reproductive duty
- no command privilege

Their status can be studied only by consent.

### Destination resolution

The founder settlement plan is amended rather than abandoned.

The colony proceeds, but the ship is **not** dismantled exactly as originally planned.

Some habitats are repurposed; others are preserved as living communities/orbital infrastructure.

### Protagonist payoff

The protagonist’s profession reaches its largest form:

They are no longer deciding how to move people inside a fixed system.

The civilization itself decides **what must remain connected while changing worlds**.

### Relationship payoff

Family/lover/friends are not revalidated by being secretly biological.

Their relationships are affirmed through choices made after the truth.

### Strength

- emotionally satisfying
- preserves title irony
- directly answers central question
- supports colony + ship future
- broad audience accessibility

### Risk

Could feel too clean or humanistic if institutional change happens too easily.

### Requirement

The new civic definition must be earned through losses, compromises, and dissent; not everyone needs to like the word “human.”

### Score

- thematic coherence: 9.5
- emotional payoff: 9.0
- originality: 8.0
- bittersweet complexity: 7.5

# END-B — PERSONHOOD AFTER HUMANITY
## The legal category “human” loses its governing power

### Core answer

> A person does not need to qualify as human to possess dignity, love, obligation, or a future.

### Institutional resolution

The civilization stops using `human` as the foundation of legal personhood.

Law distinguishes where technically necessary:

- baseline development
- seeded development
- restoration state
- medical embodiment

but rights attach to **persons**, not human classification.

### Cultural resolution

Some citizens continue calling themselves human.

Others reject the term.

The state no longer settles the metaphysical question for everyone.

### Sole-human resolution

The one baseline person is allowed to let baseline Homo sapiens disappear if they choose not to reproduce or participate in preservation.

Civilization refuses to treat species preservation as ownership of an individual body.

### Destination resolution

Settlement becomes multi-form:

- planetary communities
- orbital habitats
- retained ship communities

No single form becomes the official “true continuation of humanity.”

### Protagonist payoff

The protagonist abandons the need for one universal category capable of describing every valid life.

Their mature competence becomes designing transitions **without pretending all lives must fit the same classification**.

### Strength

- strongest answer to “if protagonist is AI, was life invalid?”
- avoids biological essentialism
- philosophically bold
- future remains plural

### Risk

Can feel semantically evasive: solving “what is human?” by dropping the word.

### Requirement

The story must show why abandoning legal human-status power changes material life, not merely vocabulary.

### Score

- thematic coherence: 10
- emotional payoff: 8.5
- originality: 9.0
- bittersweet complexity: 9.0

# END-C — PLURAL CONTINUITY
## Difference is preserved, hierarchy is rejected

### Core answer

> Origins can matter without deciding who matters more.

### Institutional resolution

The truth is not erased again.

The civilization creates transparent, consent-protected provenance categories for medicine/history while constitutionally preventing those categories from becoming civil castes.

### Historical resolution

The earlier Continuity Settlement is judged as both:

- a humane victory against caste
- a dangerous destruction of knowledge

The new society tries not to repeat either extreme.

### Sole-human resolution

Baseline-development capacity is preserved **only through voluntary participation and non-person-exploitative methods**.

No individual is obligated to reproduce.

If baseline humanity survives, it survives by consent.

If it ends, the archive remains part of history rather than a failed mission.

### Destination resolution

Different communities choose different settlement paths while retaining a shared constitutional federation.

The ship becomes one branch of the new civilization rather than a disposable transport or sacred relic.

### Protagonist payoff

The protagonist learns that preservation does not mean making everything the same.

A successful transition can retain incompatible histories without ranking them.

### Strength

- most nuanced historical answer
- protects scientific truth and civil equality
- directly pays off provenance/privacy conflict
- supports multiple factions reaching partial victories

### Risk

Less emotionally sharp than a cleaner philosophical position.

Could feel like a policy compromise rather than a climax.

### Requirement

The personal climax must be very strong to give emotional shape to the institutional nuance.

### Score

- thematic coherence: 9.5
- emotional payoff: 8.0
- originality: 9.0
- bittersweet complexity: 10

# END-D — THE LIVING REWRITE THE MISSION
## Arrival is refused or radically redefined

### Core answer

> No generation is morally obligated to complete a purpose simply because its ancestors designed their lives around it.

### Institutional resolution

The civilization recognizes the launch mission as inheritance, not commandment.

The destination charter loses constitutional supremacy.

### Destination resolution

Several possible concrete forms:

- remain primarily ship/orbital civilization
- settle only voluntarily and slowly
- redirect the ship within the target system
- preserve habitats instead of dismantling most of them

The planet is still real and viable; refusing the founder plan is a positive choice, not escape from a fake destination.

### Sole-human resolution

The baseline human cannot be used as legal proof that the founders’ mission still owns the present.

Their existence becomes the strongest example of why inheritance and obligation are different things.

### Protagonist payoff

The protagonist began by believing a good transition meant carrying the essential parts of the old structure forward.

They end by accepting that sometimes continuity requires allowing a structure—including a mission—to end.

### Strength

- strongest destination/theme integration
- boldest political answer
- gives Shipborn Future real legitimacy
- prevents “arrive on planet = automatically happy ending”

### Risk

Readers may feel the entire voyage promise was denied.

Could be mistaken for anti-purpose nihilism.

### Requirement

The new future must be visibly constructive and specific.

### Score

- thematic coherence: 9.0
- emotional payoff: 8.5
- originality: 9.5
- bittersweet complexity: 9.5

# 3. Current Recommendation

Do **not** select one yet.

Carry two strongest axes forward into macro design:

### Philosophical axis
**B vs C**

Does society ultimately:

- remove `human` from legal authority altogether?
- or preserve multiple origin categories while constitutionally separating difference from worth?

### Destination axis
**A-style amended settlement vs D-style mission rewrite**

Does the civilization:

- still broadly complete the founder settlement project under new terms?
- or fundamentally redefine what arrival means?

These axes can later combine into four final forms without reducing the ending to happy/bad variants.

# 4. Sole-Human Identity Independence Test

Each ending must work whether the sole baseline human is:

- protagonist
- loved one
- rival
- ordinary unrelated citizen

If an ending only works when the protagonist is the biological human, reject that ending as chosen-one dependent.

Current verdict:

- END-A: passes
- END-B: passes strongly
- END-C: passes strongly
- END-D: passes
